# Proof

Let \(n\in\mathbb Z\) be arbitrary, and write
\[
x=n^3-n.
\]

## 1. Factorization

Expanding the product explicitly,
\[
\begin{aligned}
n(n-1)(n+1)
&=n\bigl((n-1)(n+1)\bigr)\\
&=n(n^2+n-n-1)\\
&=n(n^2-1)\\
&=n^3-n.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\[
x=n^3-n=n(n-1)(n+1).
\]

## 2. Divisibility by \(2\)

The integers \(n-1\) and \(n\) are consecutive, so one is even. More explicitly, Euclidean division of \(n\) by \(2\) gives one of the following cases.

- If \(n=2k\) for some \(k\in\mathbb Z\), then
  \[
  x=(n-1)(2k)(n+1)
   =2\bigl(k(n-1)(n+1)\bigr).
  \]
  Thus the integer \(k(n-1)(n+1)\) is a quotient witness for \(2\mid x\).

- Otherwise \(n=2k+1\), so \(n-1=2k\), and
  \[
  x=(2k)n(n+1)
   =2\bigl(kn(n+1)\bigr).
  \]
  Thus the integer \(kn(n+1)\) is a quotient witness for \(2\mid x\).

Hence there exists \(a\in\mathbb Z\) such that
\[
x=2a.
\]

## 3. Divisibility by \(3\)

The factors \(n-1,n,n+1\) are three consecutive integers. Euclidean division of \(n\) by \(3\) gives \(n=3k+s\), where \(k\in\mathbb Z\) and \(s\in\{0,1,2\}\).

- If \(s=0\), then \(n=3k\), and
  \[
  x=(n-1)(3k)(n+1)
   =3\bigl(k(n-1)(n+1)\bigr).
  \]

- If \(s=1\), then \(n-1=3k\), and
  \[
  x=(3k)n(n+1)
   =3\bigl(kn(n+1)\bigr).
  \]

- If \(s=2\), then \(n+1=3(k+1)\), and
  \[
  x=(n-1)n\,3(k+1)
   =3\bigl((k+1)(n-1)n\bigr).
  \]

Each expression in parentheses is an integer. Hence there exists \(b\in\mathbb Z\) such that
\[
x=3b.
\]

## 4. Recombination into divisibility by \(6\)

We now have explicit equations
\[
x=2a,\qquad x=3b
\]
for integers \(a,b\). The coprimality of \(2\) and \(3\) is witnessed by the Bézout identity
\[
1=3-2.
\]
Multiplying this identity by \(a\), and using \(2a=x=3b\), gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
a
&=3a-2a\\
&=3a-x\\
&=3a-3b\\
&=3(a-b).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus \(a=3c\) for the explicit integer
\[
c=a-b\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Consequently,
\[
x=2a=2(3c)=6c=6(a-b).
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{6\mid n^3-n}.
\]

This also explains fully why simultaneous divisibility by \(2\) and \(3\) implies divisibility by \(6\) here: their Bézout identity forces the quotient \(a=x/2\) itself to be divisible by \(3\), producing the integer quotient \(c=a-b\) for division of \(x\) by \(6\).

## 5. Zero and negative integers

The argument began with an arbitrary \(n\in\mathbb Z\), not merely a positive integer. Euclidean division by the positive integers \(2\) and \(3\) applies to negative integers as well, and all quotient witnesses above are products and differences of integers. Thus they remain integers when \(n<0\).

Zero is also included directly:
\[
0^3-0=0=6\cdot 0.
\]
Hence the proof covers every integer, including zero and all negative integers.

# Independent residue-class certificate modulo \(6\)

This is a separate verification that does not depend on the consecutive-factor argument.

For every \(n\in\mathbb Z\), including negative \(n\), Euclidean division by \(6\) gives integers \(q,r\) such that
\[
n=6q+r,\qquad r\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}.
\]
Thus \(n-r=6q\). The difference of cubes satisfies
\[
n^3-r^3=(n-r)(n^2+nr+r^2)
       =6q(n^2+nr+r^2),
\]
so \(6\mid n^3-r^3\). Since \(6\mid n-r\), subtraction gives
\[
(n^3-n)-(r^3-r)=(n^3-r^3)-(n-r),
\]
which is also divisible by \(6\). Equivalently,
\[
n^3-n\equiv r^3-r\pmod 6.
\]

The six possible residues give:

| \(r\) | \(r^3-r\) | Explicit multiple of \(6\) | Residue modulo \(6\) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| \(0\) | \(0\) | \(6\cdot0\) | \(0\) |
| \(1\) | \(0\) | \(6\cdot0\) | \(0\) |
| \(2\) | \(6\) | \(6\cdot1\) | \(0\) |
| \(3\) | \(24\) | \(6\cdot4\) | \(0\) |
| \(4\) | \(60\) | \(6\cdot10\) | \(0\) |
| \(5\) | \(120\) | \(6\cdot20\) | \(0\) |

Therefore \(r^3-r\equiv0\pmod6\) in every possible residue class. The transfer above then gives
\[
n^3-n\equiv0\pmod6
\]
for every integer \(n\). Hence, independently,
\[
\boxed{6\mid n^3-n\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb Z}.
\]
